We Got The Reward Ladder Wrong — The Correction

Aug 12, 2026 · Updated Aug 14, 2026
Correction, 14 August 2026. An earlier version of this article said the 12 August update halved Legendary supply and cost around 500 trainers a tier. Both claims were wrong. Legendary supply roughly doubled, and no band lost out. The original text is gone rather than quietly patched, and what follows is the corrected analysis. We’ve explained the mistake at the end.

On 12 August Yakkamon rewrote the airdrop ladder, and on 14 August published the monster names behind it. Taken together the picture is clear, and it isn’t the one we described first time.

If you just want the analysis of the change itself rather than our correction, the full breakdown is here.

1. The four Genesis Legendaries

The founding set now has names, and the old “Legendary Egg A/B/C” labels are retired. The mapping matters, because it’s what makes the change legible:

Supply figures are official. The airdrop totals 5,010 Monster NFTs across 5,000 trainers — the extra ten are the second monsters going to ranks 1–10.

2. Legendary supply doubled

This is the number we got backwards, and it’s the heart of the update.

Before 12 AugNowChange
Legendary monsters1,0002,010+1,010
Rare Eggs4,0003,000−1,000

Legendary monsters used to stop at rank 1,000. They now run to rank 2,000 — twice as far down the leaderboard. A thousand trainers who were in line for a Rare Egg now hold a Genesis Legendary instead.

3. Nobody lost a band

We checked every band against the original ladder. Not one came out worse.

1,200 ranks improved. 3,800 held their slot with utility lifted. Zero moved down.

So “nothing has been nerfed” holds up. We questioned that framing when the bands first changed, on the basis that it was a claim about power made about an update we read as being about scarcity. With the names published, the claim is simply accurate — and the scarcity moved in players’ favour rather than against them.

4. Storm is the real story

The genuinely new thing here is a monster that sits above the previous top tier.

Until now the site’s position — from the team’s own statements — was that the Legendaries are all the same power level, differing in species and role rather than strength. Storm is the exception. It’s described as the newest in the set and the hardest to earn, and the only route to being airdropped one is a top-50 finish.

That creates something the old ladder didn’t have: a genuine gradient at the very top. Previously ranks 1 and 99 received an identical reward, which made the difference between them meaningless. Now the top 50 is a distinct prize and the top 10 is distinct again.

It also quietly addresses a design weakness we flagged. After the first dev stream we argued the flat top-100 band was a real problem. Adding a scarcer monster above the ladder fixes it without breaking the same-power-level design that the other three rest on.

5. What it means for your target

  • The Legendary line moved out, not in. It was rank 1,000. It’s now rank 2,000 — a materially easier target than before, and the single most useful change for anyone planning.
  • Top 50 is the new stretch goal, and the only way to a Storm.
  • Top 5,000 still matters — it’s the last band that receives an airdrop at all.
  • Early access is unchanged at the top 100,000, and remains very reachable free.

The flat zones are worth knowing, because points spent inside a band buy nothing: 501–2,000 all receive Tide, and 2,001–5,000 all receive a Rare Egg. Deposit timing →

6. What we still don’t know

Monster utilities land in September. Everything about what these creatures actually do — battle, farm utility, or something else — is unpublished. “Utility lifted” is the team’s description and can’t be verified until then.

It also isn’t stated whether the two monsters at ranks 1–10 are separate creatures or one reward described in two parts, though the phrasing points at separate.

How we got it wrong

Worth explaining, because it’s the second correction on this site in a fortnight and you should know how much weight to put on our numbers.

The 12 August update published bands without monster names. It listed ranks 501–2,000 as receiving “Rare Egg A” and 2,001–5,000 as “Rare Egg B”. We read “Rare” as a tier below Legendary — which it plainly implies — and concluded that ranks 501–1,000 had dropped out of the Legendary bracket, and that Legendary supply had roughly halved.

The names published on 14 August show that band receives Tide, a Genesis Legendary. Our reading was wrong, and the arithmetic built on it was wrong with it.

In hindsight the lesson is a simple one: we ran the numbers confidently on labels we didn’t understand, when the honest move was to say the labels were ambiguous and wait. We’d rather publish this than leave the original up.
